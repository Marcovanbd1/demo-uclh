Because the patient is being sent home actively responsible for several devices rather than fully recovered, the Afternote states the safety netting in full, plain, everyday language near the end of the note where it will be read closest to the moment of leaving, and frames ongoing device management as expected rather than a sign that something has gone wrong.

Psychology pass

Tone Gradient moves clearly across the record, from a planned elective operation, through an unplanned and serious deterioration involving ICU transfer, a bowel leak and a discussion of stoma surgery, to a stabilising and improving picture by discharge. Because of that arc, the middle of the Afternote is written steadily and without softening, naming the leak and the possibility of a stoma plainly, while the later stages shift in register to match the calmer, more settled state the notes actually describe.

Disclosure Readiness is present at one specific point: the entry for 9/01/2135 records that the patient was told directly about the leak and the option of a stoma, and that he expressed his own wish against further surgery. Because this was something he was recorded as consciously told and consciously decided, the Afternote writes it back to him as his own decision rather than as information being delivered, which is different from how earlier, more disorientating parts of the admission are handled.

Relational Load has a thin but real signal: no family or carer is mentioned anywhere in the record, yet the patient is discharged with several devices he must manage alone at home. Because there is no carer named in the notes, the Afternote does not assume one and instead speaks directly to what the patient himself will be responsible for.

Second, the follow up appointments are recorded only as internal clinic codes with no explanation of what they are for, so the Afternote translates these into their evident plain purpose, a scan review and a further clinic appointment, without inventing detail the notes do not contain.

There is no documented referral to or offer of psychological support anywhere in the record, despite a significant conversation about further surgery. Because nothing of that kind is recorded, the Afternote does not raise psychological support at all, rather than naming an absence that was never tested for.

Temporal pressure

The record contains an extensive list of organisms, antibiotic changes and individual scan findings that were necessary for the clinical team managing the admission but carry no decision weight for the patient tonight. These have been cut. What remains is what is true and actionable at the moment of reading: the devices he is going home with, the fluid, diet and activity guidance, the safety netting, and the specific dates already booked for his GP review and follow up appointments.

Afternote

Your stay, from surgery to home

A short account of what happened during your admission, written for you, from your operation through to leaving hospital.

Surgery day

Your operation and the days after

You had a planned operation to remove your bladder, along with the nearby lymph nodes, and to build a new way for urine to be stored and passed, known as a neobladder. During the operation there was some contamination from your bowel, so you were started straight away on antibiotics given into a vein.

Because it was difficult to keep a line in your arm, you were moved to the intensive care unit and a tube was placed into a larger vein in your neck, so you could keep receiving fluids and medication safely.

The weeks that followed

When things did not go smoothly

In the days after your operation, your bowel stopped working properly and you developed pain and vomiting. A tube was passed through your nose into your stomach to relieve the pressure while things settled, and you needed regular washouts through your bladder catheter to keep it clear. Once you were not able to eat enough on your own, you were fed for a time through a line directly into your bloodstream.

Scans over the following weeks showed that your bowel was becoming blocked, and that fluid was leaking from your new bladder into the area around it. You developed a temperature, and infection was found in fluid draining from the area, so your antibiotics were changed more than once as the team worked out what would treat it best.

When later scans confirmed a small leak in your bowel joining onto the leak from your new bladder, the surgical team spoke with you directly and explained that one option would be to form a stoma. You told them clearly that you did not want another operation. Together you agreed a plan to manage things with drains instead, giving your body the chance to heal on its own. That was your decision, and it shaped everything that came after it.

Getting steadier

Things starting to settle

A line was placed in your arm so you could keep being fed through your bloodstream while your bowel recovered. Your antibiotics were changed again as you improved, moving eventually from injections to tablets. You gradually returned to eating a normal diet, your blood tests looked reassuring, and your vital signs stayed stable. On that basis, your team felt you were ready to go home.

Home

Going home with your drains and stents

You are going home with your surgical drains still in place, the stents draining your kidneys into your new bladder, and your catheter still in. You have already been shown how to manage all of these. This does not mean anything has gone wrong. It means your body needs a bit more time before everything can safely come out.

Over the next few weeks, try to drink around two litres of fluid a day, eat plenty of fibre, fruit and vegetables, and build your activity back up gradually with short walks. Try not to strain when you open your bowels. Avoid any heavy lifting, driving or strenuous exercise for four weeks, and avoid fizzy or caffeinated drinks for six weeks.

Come back to hospital straight away if you develop a fever or chills, feel generally unwell, become unusually sleepy, start vomiting, feel more tired than normal, notice any signs of infection, or if one of your stents comes out. If you go to a different hospital, take this summary with you so they know exactly what has happened.

You have a blood test and review with your GP booked for one week from now, mainly to check for any early signs of infection. You also have a scan booked for around four weeks to look at how the leak is healing, followed by a review of that scan, and a further clinic appointment in four to six weeks.
